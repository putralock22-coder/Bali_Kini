Bali's Special Committee on Spatial Planning, Regional Assets, and Licensing (Pansus TRAP) at the provincial legislature has recommended temporary closure and, in some cases, permanent demolition of several tourism buildings along the cliffs of Pecatu village, South Kuta district, Badung regency. The recommendation follows findings of a recurring pattern of zoning, environmental, and licensing violations that lawmakers described as "brutal and reckless" along Bali's southern cliff line.

## A Repeating Pattern of Violations

During hearings held this week, Pansus TRAP summoned management representatives from four tourism projects to provide clarification: Rockfish Uluwatu, Stone Villa Uluwatu, Dream Project, and Nova Ocean Hotel, operated by PT MAS. Committee member Dewa Nyoman Rai said the violation pattern found was nearly identical across all the projects examined — construction on cliff faces and ravines, in disaster-mitigation zones, and even on land officially designated as Protected Rice Fields (LSD) or Sustainable Food Agricultural Land (LP2B).

"If violations are proven, our recommendation is clear: demolish," Rai said, according to minutes from the committee hearing.

The Pecatu case is not an isolated one. Badung's southern cliff belt — stretching from Pecatu and Uluwatu to Suluban — has repeatedly drawn scrutiny from Pansus TRAP since earlier this year over a wave of tourism accommodation projects cutting into cliff faces without complete permits. Similar cases previously ensnared Hotel The Edge and Hotel Mulia in the Pecatu and Nusa Dua areas, both investigated over allegations of operating for years without valid licenses.

## Three Official Recommendations

Pansus TRAP delivered three formal recommendations to the executive branches of the Bali provincial government and Badung regency government. First, the Civil Service Police Unit (Satpol PP) was asked to coordinate with relevant regional agencies to conduct in-depth investigations of all construction activity in disaster-prone zones, cliffs, and ravines — covering spatial-plan compliance, land-use appropriateness, and the legality of every permit already issued.

Second, the committee urged the temporary suspension of all business and construction activity that has not met full licensing requirements. Third, buildings proven to violate regulations — particularly those standing in disaster-mitigation zones such as cliff edges and steep ravines — were recommended for permanent demolition in the interest of public safety and environmental preservation.

## Implications for Bali's Zoning and Tourism Sector

The Pecatu case is emerging as a key test of how consistently Bali enforces its spatial-planning rules amid a surge of premium accommodation investment that trades on cliff and ocean views as its main selling point. Over the past decade, the Uluwatu and Pecatu area has grown rapidly into a high-end hospitality destination, but that growth has often outpaced compliance with the Detailed Spatial Plan (RDTR) and required landslide and coastal-erosion mitigation studies.

If the demolition recommendation is actually carried out, the impact will be felt directly by operators who invested without securing complete permits, and it would set a precedent for dozens of similar projects scattered along Bali's southern cliffs — from Pecatu and Uluwatu to Suluban and Nusa Dua. Local residents and environmental advocates hope the recommendation does not stay on paper, noting that past zoning-violation cases in Bali have often failed to result in firm enforcement action on the ground.

The Badung regency and Bali provincial governments now face public pressure to follow through transparently, including releasing the results of the Satpol PP investigation and a timeline for demolishing any buildings confirmed to be in violation.
